# Riley — second-opinion playtest, 2026-11-18

Riley finished all six levels and the rules card, then played The Wayfarer for **10 minutes 6 seconds of actual elapsed time**, escaping twice. The little authored games and state inspector are strong teaching material. However, the recommended AI repeatedly substituted a different object for the one explicitly requested, acted on unnamed objects, and once moved Riley away from the escape pod when asked to enter it. I would not teach “it asks instead of guessing” from this build without showing those counterexamples.

**Persona:** Riley, 18, senior in a game design club; experienced with playing and making text adventures. Reads the instructions, cares about puzzle fairness, and deliberately probes the interpreter. This is a fictional persona, not a record about a real student.

## Level 2: a useful inspector, with a repeated scenery trap

### Authored puzzle path

The emergency manual explained that R-6 can reroute auxiliary power, needs a charged power cell, and spare cells are in the cargo supply crate. I examined/took it, went east to Main Corridor, checked the console (power offline, life support reserve, escape pod sealed), then east to Cargo Bay. The supply crate was jammed; opening it explained that bare hands would not work and **“something with leverage might.”** The dark droid had an empty cell socket and was unresponsive when spoken to. This is excellent actionable feedback.

Went west/south to Engine Room, examined/took prybar and examined reactor. The reactor explained that primary cells were damaged and auxiliary power was the droid's job. Returned to cargo, used prybar on supply crate, took power cell, used it on droid, then talked to droid. Inspector confirmed cell inventory→hidden and `droid_active: true`; dialogue restored power. Corridor lighting and status console changed coherently. Explicitly opening the pod bay door changed its locked/open flags; north entered Escape Pod Bay.

The pod description said it was cramped and flight-ready; lever description warned of no countdown or second chances. **`enter escape pod` returned “If you want to use the escape pod, you'll need to go into it.”** Inspector recognized a traverse action, fell through to a use rule, and fired `use_escape_pod`. This is contradictory instruction for exactly the attempted action. `use launch lever` automatically narrated strapping in and launching, so it did not block winning. First escape: 29 turns.

## Bugs, interpretation limits, and what was not tested

**Definite observed defects:** wrong-object substitutions were accepted; an enter-pod request became movement away from it; help-listed boarding phrases returned contradictory instructions; non-question error messages were labelled questions and satisfied Level 3's gate. The model is probabilistic, so this report does not claim identical output on every retry. The resulting actions and feedback were nevertheless actually observed, not inferred from source.

**Feedback/puzzle-design problems:** vines are described but denied as targets; the open urn cannot be “opened” without an already-open explanation; a present reactor is said not to be seen when addressed as a character. These may reflect deliberate object-kind rules, but their wording misrepresents the visible situation. The empty droid conversation's inspector said `npc_spoke` / dialogue fired despite an unresponsive narrative; that may simply be an event name, not a behavioral bug.

**Tools/session limits:** browser operation was relayed, level timings estimated, and one Wayfarer named-room test began from the wrong room due to operator sequencing; it was repeated from the intended room. Real ten-minute Wayfarer timing was verified. No reload, narrow-screen, school-network, keyboard-only, screen-reader, printing, or download-failure test was performed. I opened the rules card but did not print it. No claim is made about skipped-level gates or validation of junk/empty text. No material vocabulary barrier for this experienced persona; the confusing wording was inaccurate feedback, rather than jargon.

## What Riley learned and where Riley would stop

“The author makes the actual objects and rules. AI can translate words or decorate descriptions, but its structured output and its own paraphrase can disagree. The game catches some bad guesses and accepts others, so I need to inspect the actual action and test the boundaries.”

That broadly matches the intended distinction between interpreting intent and deciding truth. The strongest evidence was adding my own fixed statue and seeing aliases work. The current promise that the AI asks rather than guesses does **not** match the session. I would finish, as I did, but would stop trusting semantic movement after the pod request moved me south. As a game-maker I would switch the interpreter off to protect my progress.

## Riley's top five fixes

1. **Reject target/direction substitutions the player did not request.** Preserve explicit nouns; ask a genuine clarification for unnamed or unresolved objects. The statue→altar and pod→south cases are more serious than decorative prose errors because accepted actions can change play.
2. **Make clarification badges and Level 3 completion truthful.** A failed lookup is not a question. Validate the actual response/action outcome before saying “You got a question, not a guess”; do not echo a clear request as the clarification.
3. **Repair advertised boarding/entering actions in The Wayfarer.** `enter`, `board`, and `get in` should enter, or explicitly explain that launch will include boarding. `go into escape pod` must never move away from it.
4. **Align scenery nouns and failure wording with puzzle facts.** Support cutting the described vines, acknowledge already-open urns, and distinguish “not a character” from “not present.” These fixes improve fairness before introducing AI.
5. **Teach the interpreter's real failure modes alongside the dial.** Show the paraphrase/structured-output disagreement, distinguish interpretation from generated prose in the inspector, and include a tested incorrect-object example. The transparent inspector is a strength; use it to explain actual limits rather than a guarantee the model did not meet.
