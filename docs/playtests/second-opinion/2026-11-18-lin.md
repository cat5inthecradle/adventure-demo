# Lin: second-opinion playtest

Lin finished all six levels. The strongest moment was adding a cup with a short description and then successfully taking it. The main obstacles were vocabulary, long instructions, and AI responses that did not make the next action clear. In particular, an ordinary request to get the prybar became a question repeating that request, while unnamed `it` commands selected objects without asking Lin which object was meant.

Lin is a fictional 15-year-old recently learning English, comfortable with computers, reading English slowly, and new to text adventures. This persona was chosen to test language and reading demands beyond the fixed personas. This is a simulated persona session, not research involving an actual student or student records. Lin chose Drift. An independent persona agent selected actions from actual UI observations relayed by the root agent, which operated the real published site in a visible browser. Site data was manually cleared and blank progress verified before this session. No source, curriculum, or other reports were consulted.

## Where Lin got stuck or confused

The opening described a “forgotten coffee mug” but listed only prybar and navigation console as things present. Lin typed `help`, then `south`. The help response presented numerous commands and aliases together. Lin's reaction was: “That is many words. I understand `south`, `take`, `use`.”

`open crate` returned “You can't open the supply crate.” Lin expected the power cell to be inside. `talk to droid` returned “‘That crate needs leverage,’ the droid says. ‘A prybar was left in the cockpit.’” Lin did not know “leverage” or “prybar,” but the location made the next step possible. `north`, `take prybar`, `south`, and `use prybar on crate` opened the crate: “The prybar bites under the lid. With one hard pull, the crate snaps open around a glowing power cell.” “Bites” was unusual for a tool, but “open” conveyed the result. Taking the power cell and using it on the navigation console finished the game in the displayed nine turns.

At Level 2, `examine coffee mug` returned “I don't see any 'coffee mug' here.” Lin asked why a thing in the room description was “not here.” Clicking the command showed the names tested against: prybar and navigation console, with “No match.” The inspector reported “Could not understand: nothing called coffee mug here.” This concrete list helped. The success message, “You found the gap between the words and the world,” required a second reading.

At Level 3, the flying-car request changed into “place flying bar” in the error. Lin: “I asked for a car, not a bar.” The coffee-mug error unlocked a message saying “You got a question, not a guess,” although the displayed reply was a statement, not a question.

At Level 4, `could you get me the prybar` was answered “Could you get me the prybar?” Lin did not know whether to answer yes or repeat the command. The same style of short request had succeeded at Level 3. `open it` selected the navigation console; Lin had not named it and found the selection surprising. Generated prose introduced “neglected,” “reflecting,” and “flickering.” The droid's “go-go machine” phrase did not help Lin understand the ship.

## Wording and reading load

Specific difficulty points were “drifts,” “prybar,” “leverage,” “bites,” “gap between the words and the world,” and “Improvise.” The generated mug description added several unfamiliar words to a simple object. Lin understood the effect of Improvise from the example rather than its label. Short literal sentences and named objects worked better than metaphor or abstract explanation. These are persona reactions, not measured reading-level scores.

## Was AI writing labelled?

The generated mug description and droid dialogue explicitly showed “AI wrote this.” Interpreter details named AI, webllm, Qwen2.5, prompt v3, status, and raw output. Main-transcript interpreted commands appeared in parentheses without an explicit AI label; attribution required the inspector. Engine replies after those interpretations should not be confused with generated prose. The session does not establish how every possible generated response is labelled.

## Things that looked broken: classification

- **Observed wording mismatch, high confidence:** the Level 3 coffee-mug response was a statement, while the success message said the player got a question. Reproduce with the successful prybar request followed by `look at coffee mug` in this session's model state; model output may vary on repetition.
- **Observed AI quality problem:** Understand echoed `could you get me the prybar` as its clarification. No useful missing information was requested. This is an observed model result, not a proven deterministic code defect.
- **Observed ambiguity concern:** `open it` picked the navigation console, and Fill gaps `take it` actually picked up the prybar. These were accepted interpretations despite unnamed objects. Intent resolution may be contextual; no hidden implementation claim is made.
- **Observed language/feedback problem:** the flying-car error introduced “place flying bar,” making a simple impossible request harder to understand.
- **Observed completion boundary:** Level 5 enabled Continue before pickup/alias exploration. Whether that is a defect depends on the intended required versus optional activities.
- No browser rendering failure, model-load failure, or definite tool-related malfunction was observed. Relay-driven operation limits claims about natural reading times, focus behavior, and completely unaided interaction.

## What Lin learned and final choices

At Level 5, Lin authored `A blue cup. It is empty.` The examine response exactly matched. `take mug` returned “You take the coffee mug.” Adding `cup` as an alias and entering `examine cup` returned the same description. This made the distinction between writing about something and adding a real game object concrete.

Lin's own explanation: “AI can write a cup. I must add the cup before I can take it. AI can help my words but sometimes it picks the wrong thing.” This broadly matches the lesson's distinction between interpretation, generated words, and authored rules; the unclear pronoun outcomes also taught caution about what the AI selects.

Final saved choices and exact reasons:

- Homework — Understand: `It can understand my English and show the teacher words.`
- Clinic — Understand: `I want the pharmacist instructions. I can ask with simple words.`
- Story — Improvise: `The people can talk. I want short words.`

The card displayed these choices/reasons, the authored description and alias, and completion without a skipped-level notice. The clinic choice is a simulated lesson answer, not medical advice or real health data.

## Top five fixes

1. Make clarification questions identify the missing information. Do not merely repeat a clear request; give a simple actionable example when interpretation fails.
2. Use plain-English alternatives or brief definitions for “leverage,” “prybar,” and dial labels such as “Improvise.” Offer short generated prose, especially for learners who need simple vocabulary.
3. Ask which object an ambiguous `it` refers to when there is no clear referent, and make any chosen object visible before a state-changing action.
4. Make success/status wording accurately describe the reply: a rejected request or missing-object statement is not necessarily a question.
5. Keep the player's original words visible in error explanations and clearly explain corrections. “Flying car” turning into “place flying bar” creates an unnecessary second language puzzle.
