# Maya — second-opinion playtest, 2026-11-17

Maya finished all six levels and reached her rules card. The explicit hints and small authoring exercise worked well for a careful first-time player. The main obstacle was inconsistent AI feedback: a supplied example failed, an unnamed escape request generated a strange object error, and the inspector sometimes said the AI was not needed immediately before describing AI-generated text. She could finish with a useful understanding, but would want a teacher to explain those moments.

Maya is a fictional 13-year-old eighth grader, new to text adventures and unfamiliar with parser/interpreter terminology. This is an agent simulation, not research involving a real student. Tested the published site in Drift at 1280×800. A dedicated Maya agent chose every action; the root agent operated the real in-app browser and relayed exact visible results because the child agent could not access that browser. The root supplied a clean starting session and retained screenshots. Native Chrome setup failed earlier; that failure is excluded from lesson findings and time estimates. No source or previous playtest reports were read.

## Stuck or confused: actions, exact feedback, expectations

### Level 1

Maya followed the instruction to type `help`. The response offered looking, moving, carrying, doors, using, talking, listening, and meta commands. It was long but useful; she concentrated on the concrete commands and ignored save/pronoun/meta options.

She typed `go south`, then `talk to droid`. The droid said: “'That crate needs leverage,' the droid says. 'A prybar was left in the cockpit.'” She returned north, took the prybar, returned south and typed `use prybar on supply crate`. Response: “The prybar bites under the lid. With one hard pull, the crate snaps open around a glowing power cell.” She took the cell, returned north and used it on the navigation console. The terminal ended with “*** You have escaped. THE END ***” and “Escaped in 9 turns.” The lesson said “The ship is awake. You made it home!” No discouraging stall occurred.

### Level 2

Maya followed `examine coffee mug`, expecting to see the mug mentioned in the room. The reply was “I don't see any 'coffee mug' here.” Clicking the command revealed an object lookup containing prybar and navigation console but no mug, followed by “Could not understand: nothing called ‘coffee mug’ here.” The lesson's “You found the gap between the words and the world” made the intended surprise clear. “Could not understand” is less precise than the evidence: the verb was understood but the named object was absent.

### Levels 3–4

Full request/output records are below. The strongest confusing moments were:

- `get me out of here` produced “I don't see any 'for way' here.” Maya had never written those words. The inspector called this “asked a question” although it was a statement of failure.
- The supplied Level 4 example, `could you get me the prybar`, worked differently from Level 3's similar request: the AI replied “Could you get me the prybar?” Maya would read that as the game asking her to get the tool, reversing the roles.
- Fill gaps said “AI wrote this: You find the forgotten coffee mug, its handle cool and smooth.” “You find” sounds like the mug has become real. Later `inventory` said “You are carrying nothing.” This confirms nothing was taken, but examining an existing object would not take it either; that check alone does not prove the mug is absent.
- The inspector simultaneously displayed “AI Not needed. The game understood by itself.” and then explained that the AI filled a gap or improvised. For a novice, these descriptions contradict each other.

### Levels 5–6

Maya added description “A blue mug with a little star on it.” `examine mug` returned her exact description. `take mug` returned “You take the coffee mug.” She saved alias `cup`; `examine cup` returned the same description and the carried state remained intact. This was the clearest demonstration that authoring changes what is real.

She chose Understand for homework, Off for a clinic, and Improvise for a story game, with reasons recorded on her final card. No field-entry problem attributable to the product occurred. A locator mismatch during the operator's entry of the Level 4 reason was resolved with normal accessibility typing and is a tool issue, not a finding about the lesson.

## Wording Maya might not understand

- “AI Interpreter” is initially unexplained vocabulary for her; the visible recommended model choice helps her proceed without knowing it.
- “leverage” and “prybar” are potentially unfamiliar, but the droid names the location and the use action produces concrete feedback, so neither blocked her.
- “Could not understand” conflates an unknown object with an unknown command in the Level 2 explanation.
- “AI Not needed. The game understood by itself.” becomes confusing when paired with “AI filled a gap” or “AI improvised.”

These are simulated persona interpretations; they are not claims that a real student reported those difficulties.

## Was AI-written text labelled?

Fill gaps and Improvise prose explicitly began “AI wrote this:” and the inspector also described the generation. In Level 3 the displayed interpretation `(take the prybar)` was not explicitly labelled AI in the main reply; the selected model and inspector provided attribution. The Level 4 AI clarification “Could you get me the prybar?” likewise was not reported with an inline AI label. Thus not all AI-derived text had the same explicit inline attribution. Game-authored action replies should not be mistaken for AI prose.

## Broken behavior and reproduction confidence

**Observed product defects, high confidence in the visible inconsistency:**

1. In Understand, enter `get me out of here`, then open its command inspector. In this run it showed an error about `for way` and labelled a declarative refusal “asked a question.” The exact AI output is stochastic, but this occurred with the recommended model.
2. In Fill gaps, `examine the mug`, or in Improvise, `ask the droid about the ship`, then inspect the command. The inspector's “AI Not needed” statement conflicts with its later AI generation explanation. These are user-visible contradictory explanations, regardless of internal stage distinctions.

**Observed model failure, not established as a deterministic software bug:**

3. In Level 4 under Understand, the supplied `could you get me the prybar` example merely echoed a question. This demonstrates an unreliable example in this run; it does not establish that every run fails.

**Observed completion behavior, intent uncertain:**

4. Level 5 Continue unlocked before take/alias tasks; Level 4 completed despite the unsuccessful Understand demonstration. These are reproducible observations in this session, but whether they violate intended gating is not known.

**Tool/environment limits:** Child browser access and earlier native app timeouts were automation limitations. A field locator mismatch was resolved by the operator and is not a site bug. No WebGPU/download limitation occurred in the successful run. School-device performance, printing, keyboard-only use, reload persistence, world switching, and navigation bypass were not tested by Maya.

## What Maya would say she learned

> “The AI can turn my sentence into a game command, but it can guess wrong. The game can say no to that guess. A mug in the story isn't something I can pick up until the person making the game adds it. I would let AI make characters talk, but not decide medicine.”

That broadly matches the intended boundary between interpretation, authored truth and game rules. The moon rejection and authoring exercise support it directly. The inspector contradictions and changing resolution of “it” make the precise mechanics harder to explain confidently.

Final choices: homework Understand — “I want help understanding the question but I don't want it making up the answer.” Clinic Off — “Medicine needs to be right and the doctor should choose it.” Story Improvise — “It can make the characters talk but I want the rules to stay the same.” The final card retained these answers, the Level 4 boundary reason, mug description and cup alias. “You finished the lesson.” was visible. Print my card was present but not clicked.

## Maya's top five fixes

1. Make supplied natural-language examples reliable, or explicitly explain a failed/clarifying outcome and give a recovery action. The failed Level 4 example is the point a careful student could blame herself.
2. Replace malformed paraphrase errors such as “for way” with an understandable clarification; classify declarative failures correctly instead of “asked a question.”
3. Make the inspector distinguish interpretation from generated prose so “AI Not needed” cannot contradict “AI filled a gap/improvised.”
4. Strengthen the fake-mug comparison with a direct attempt to take it before authoring; explicitly distinguish descriptive words from real interactable objects. Preserve the successful authoring exercise.
5. Align visible completion with all requested steps, or mark take/alias and AI demonstrations as optional. Level 5 currently permits Continue before two requested actions.
