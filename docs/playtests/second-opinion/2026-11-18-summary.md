# Adventure Lab — second opinion, November 17–18, 2026

The lesson is promising for a teacher-led pilot, but **not ready for broad classroom use with its current claims about AI intent and completion**. Three things are most likely to go wrong: **AI executes a different action/object/direction than requested; misleading questions and a failed supplied example confuse careful readers; a final card can omit essential AI experience or contain nonsense reasons.** These are observed outcomes, not estimated model failure rates.

The strongest part is Level 5: authoring an object, description, aliases and pickup rule concretely demonstrates that people decide game facts. Keep that exercise and the inspectable action history. Observed Fill gaps/Improvise narrative was labelled “AI wrote this.” Hints and the small puzzles generally supported progress.

## Sessions and method

Five independent persona agents chose actions and wrote reports without reading source, curriculum, earlier reports or each other's findings. Root operated the real published site as a browser relay because child agents could not access this chat's in-app browser. These are fictional simulations, not research with actual students. The site passed the six-level Learn / Play / Benchmark version check at 1280×800. No local server was used.

The user authorized visible in-app sessions with manual site-data clearing instead of private profiles. Blank final answers were verified before subsequent personas. One reported reset initially retained Dev's answers; Riley waited for another clear and verification. Returning to Level 1, changing worlds and reloading demonstrably did not clear all answers. Downloaded-model cache isolation was not established.

## What could not be tested

- Actual student comprehension, classroom timing, teacher workload or prevalence. Relay affects pace and loses interaction nuance.
- Chromebook/low-memory GPU support, locked-down school networks, every cold download or a genuine five-minute download/WebGPU failure. Recommended Qwen2.5 1.5B (~1.7 GB) worked in four AI-enabled runs; Sam never started a download.
- Screen readers, a full accessibility audit, physical keyboard behavior independent of automation, touch/narrow screens. Sam's selector issue is not a confirmed accessibility defect.
- Physical printing or print layout; cards were displayed and captured. Not every world/dial combination or repeated stochastic output was tested.
- Private-profile isolation and persistence internals. User-cleared storage plus visible blank answers were used as authorized.

No source files were changed and no commit was made. Existing unrelated working-tree changes were left untouched. All five independent reports and this independent synthesis were written before the earlier-round reports were opened. Individual findings were not rewritten to match them.


## Compared with the earlier round

Read after the independent reports were saved: [first-timer](../2026-11-16-round3-first-timer.md), [skimmer](../2026-11-16-round3-skimmer.md), and [gamer](../2026-11-16-round3-gamer.md). Those sessions used localhost ports 5186–5188 on November 16; ours used the published site on November 17–18. Without source/build comparison, differences cannot be attributed confidently to fixes, regressions or model variability.

**Agreement:** The earlier team already found accepted unrelated listen/use/take/movement actions, misleading paraphrase errors, unreliable Level 4 examples, weak reflection completion, partial state resets and strong prose-versus-object teaching. Our independent evidence supports those concerns. Accepted legal actions are not evidence that AI rewrote game rules: both rounds show the engine executing allowed commands that misrepresent player intent. The shared strengths are labelled generated narrative, useful inspectors, hints and authorship. Both rounds leave cold school-device model loading and printing unresolved.

**Additional evidence:** Sam supplies a fully keyboard-driven path through game, inspector, authoring and reflection, plus an unresolved native model-menu limitation and Level 4 completion without a model. Lin exposes vocabulary and malformed corrections as a separate language-learning burden. Riley's statue-to-altar substitution extends the prior mug-to-console example to Temple; his pod-entry request actually moved away from the destination, followed by contradictory help-listed boarding commands. These specific boarding cases were absent from the earlier reports. Level 5's pickup restriction was previously described but not exercised by the earlier gamer; Riley now verified an authored fixed statue could not be taken. The repeated “AI Not needed” versus AI-generation explanation and explicitly counted false-question completion receive stronger cross-persona evidence here.

**Different observations:** Current Dev's blank Level 5 description triggered “Please fill out this field,” and repeated junk reasons were rejected, unlike earlier skimmer claims. Varied nonsense still passed Level 6. Current cards explicitly counted skipped levels rather than claiming the whole lesson was finished. Current Sam saw a clear no-model warning on Level 4; the earlier skimmer reported much less visible feedback after reload. The earlier example was `pick up the tool on the floor`; ours was `could you get me the prybar/machete`. Changing the example did not yield a successful contrast in three runs. We did not retest every old input, cross-world reason transfer, alias collisions or invented mechanics in descriptions, so those are unresolved rather than declared fixed.

**Where the earlier conclusions overreach or I disagree:**

- The earlier skimmer calls an empty description accepted, but its reproduction says an earlier description remained in the field and was returned on examination. That evidence does not establish a truly empty submitted value. Our explicit blank submission was blocked.
- The earlier first-timer infers what a screen reader would announce from unnamed buttons and duplicated page text. That is a useful accessibility suspicion, but page extraction alone cannot establish spoken behavior. Sam observed named world controls in the current accessibility view; neither this keyboard-only run nor those earlier observations constitute a screen-reader test.
- The earlier gamer's “Fixed for the gate” conclusion is too broad: its own table includes a non-question classified as a question. Our repeated Level 3 false-success cases show why merely unlocking Continue is insufficient evidence of a corrected teaching gate.
- I would not prioritize rejecting an Invent choice in the hypothetical clinic scenario as a categorical correctness check. The activity explicitly asks learners to defend a choice. The stronger supported problem is accepting unrelated reasons without meaningful reflection; teacher discussion or consequence-focused prompts can address that without silently imposing one answer.
- The earlier gamer calls Level 5 the weakest level and recommends more mechanics. Our careful beginner, English learner and keyboard-only persona found the small authorship exercise the clearest learning moment; Riley's fixed-object test was also strong. More scope may help advanced players, but preserving this accessible core is the higher priority.

The earlier positive game-club recommendation is compatible with a supervised pilot, but is not enough evidence for general classroom readiness. Neither round measured real student outcomes or target-school hardware reliability.
